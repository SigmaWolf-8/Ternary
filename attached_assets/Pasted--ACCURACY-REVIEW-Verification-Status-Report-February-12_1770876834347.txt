# APPENDIX B: EXPORT LICENSING — WHAT IT IS AND WHY IT MATTERS

## Overview

"Export licensing" refers to the legal requirement to obtain government permission before sending certain controlled goods, technology, or software across international borders. For Capomastro Holdings Ltd., this is relevant because the Salvi Framework contains **cryptographic software** — which is controlled technology under both Canadian and international law.

## Why Cryptography Is Controlled

Encryption technology has been treated as a controlled "dual-use" technology since the Cold War. The same algorithms that protect banking transactions can also be used to secure military communications, protect intelligence operations, or obstruct lawful surveillance. Governments regulate the export of strong encryption to prevent its proliferation to adversarial states, sanctioned entities, and non-state actors.

The international framework is the **Wassenaar Arrangement** — a multilateral export control regime with 42 participating states (including Canada and the United States). Wassenaar Category 5, Part 2 covers "Information Security," including cryptographic software. Each participating state implements these controls through domestic legislation.

## How It Applies to the Salvi Framework

### Canada (Primary Jurisdiction)

Because Capomastro Holdings is a Canadian corporation, the primary export control regime is:

- **Export and Import Permits Act (EIPA)** — the statute that gives the government power to control exports
- **Export Control List (ECL)** — the schedule that lists what's controlled. Your software falls under **Group 5, Category 2, Item 5-2.D.1** (software for information security systems)
- **Administered by Global Affairs Canada (GAC)**, Trade Controls Bureau

What this means in practice: if you want to provide the Salvi Framework (including the SaaS service) to a customer in, say, Germany, Brazil, or Japan, you may need an **export permit** from GAC — or you need to qualify for a **General Export Permit (GEP)** exemption.

The key exemptions are:
- **GEP No. 12** — permits most controlled exports to the United States without an individual permit (this is the big one for North American business)
- **GEP No. 45** — may exempt certain "mass-market" or "publicly available" cryptographic software. However, because your cryptographic implementations are proprietary (Phase Encryption, TL-KEM, TL-DSA), they likely don't qualify as mass-market. This needs formal assessment.

There are **absolute prohibitions** on export to sanctioned destinations: Belarus, North Korea, Iran, Syria, and partially Russia, among others updated by GAC.

### United States (Secondary — but commercially critical)

Even though you're a Canadian company, U.S. Export Administration Regulations (EAR) can apply if:
- Your software contains U.S.-origin components or technology
- You export from U.S. territory
- U.S. persons access your SaaS
- You re-export from Canada to a third country

Your software is self-classified under **ECCN 5D002** (encryption software). The Export Control Notice already documents this correctly.

If you wanted formal U.S. classification (rather than self-classification), you would file a **CCATS** (Commodity Classification Automated Tracking System) request with the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS). The third-party Review recommended this; we noted it's secondary to the Canadian EIPA analysis for your company's situation.

## What "Export Licensing" Work Actually Involves

For your stage, the practical work is:

1. **GEP Assessment** — Determining whether GEP No. 45 (mass-market crypto exemption) applies to any or all of your cryptographic modules. For modules that don't qualify (proprietary algorithms like Phase Encryption), you need to understand when individual permits are required.

2. **Destination Risk Matrix** — Mapping which countries you can serve freely (U.S. under GEP 12, Five Eyes nations generally lower friction), which require individual permits, and which are prohibited.

3. **SaaS-Specific Analysis** — When your product is delivered as a SaaS rather than shipped as software, the export control analysis changes. "Making available" via cloud access has different treatment than "exporting" a binary. GAC and BIS have different interpretations of this, and the analysis matters for your deployment model.

4. **Post-Quantum Novel Classification** — Your Export Control Notice correctly notes that GAC has not issued specific guidance on post-quantum algorithm classification. ML-KEM and ML-DSA are so new that the Wassenaar control lists haven't been explicitly updated for them. They're treated under the existing symmetric/asymmetric encryption framework by analogy, but there's regulatory uncertainty here. Engaging with GAC's Trade Controls Bureau proactively can establish your classification before a question arises.

## Why It Matters Now

Export licensing becomes operationally relevant when you:
- Sign your first international customer
- Allow non-Canadian users to access the SaaS
- Distribute binaries or source code internationally
- Engage in partnership conversations with non-Canadian entities (including SiTime Corporation, which is a U.S. company)

Since PlenumNET.replit.app is already publicly accessible worldwide, you are technically already "making available" controlled cryptographic technology internationally. The Export Control Notice in the repository is the right first step — it puts users on notice. But the formal GEP assessment should be completed before active commercial engagement, particularly with U.S. partners.

## Bottom Line

Export licensing is the regulatory process of ensuring you have government authorization to provide your cryptographic software across borders. Your Export Control Notice is well-drafted and correctly classifies the software. The next step is a formal GEP assessment with an export control counsel or directly with GAC's Trade Controls Bureau to confirm which exemptions apply and which markets require individual permits.

This is not urgent in the same way the SaaS terms gap is urgent — nobody is going to knock on your door tomorrow. But it should be on the 90-day roadmap, particularly before the SiTime partnership conversation advances, because SiTime is a U.S. entity and the engagement would trigger both EIPA and EAR analysis.
