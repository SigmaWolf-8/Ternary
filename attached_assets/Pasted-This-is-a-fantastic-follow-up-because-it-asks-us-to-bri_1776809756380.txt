This is a fantastic follow-up because it asks us to bridge the exact, discrete world of GCD with the continuous, asymptotic world of $x/\ln(x)$.

The "parametric test formula" that unites these two concepts is the Legendre Formula (also known as the Sieve of Eratosthenes algebraic identity). It uses the GCD condition $\text{GCD}(n, m)=1$ to exactly count primes, and then we can algebraically manipulate it to reveal why $\frac{x}{\ln(x)}$ emerges.

Step 1: The Exact Parametric Formula (The GCD Foundation)

Let the "magnitude" be $x$. Let $a$ be a parameter representing the square root bound, $a = \lfloor \sqrt{x} \rfloor$. Let $P$ be the product of all primes $p \le a$.

By the fundamental theorem of arithmetic and the definition of GCD, an integer $n \le x$ is a prime > a if and only if:

\text{GCD}(n, P) = 1

The exact count of such numbers (plus the primes $\le a$ themselves) is given by the inclusion-exclusion principle:

\pi(x) = \pi(a) - 1 + \sum_{d \mid P} \mu(d) \left\lfloor \frac{x}{d} \right\rfloor

Where:

· $d$ is a divisor of $P$ (a product of distinct primes $\le a$).
· $\mu(d)$ is the Möbius function:
  · $\mu(d) = 1$ if $d$ has an even number of prime factors.
  · $\mu(d) = -1$ if $d$ has an odd number of prime factors.
  · $\mu(d) = 0$ if $d$ has a squared prime factor (though by definition of $P$, we only use square-free $d$ here).

How to interpret this formula algebraically:
This is a parametric sieve. The parameter $a = \sqrt{x}$ determines the set of primes we test against. The $\lfloor x/d \rfloor$ term literally counts how many numbers $\le x$ share a common divisor (GCD > 1) with $d$.

---

Step 2: From the Exact Formula to the Asymptotic Limit

Now, we want to show algebraically how the above GCD test transforms into $\frac{x}{\ln(x)}$.

The floor function $\lfloor x/d \rfloor$ makes this jumpy and discrete. To find the "smooth trend" (the asymptotic law), we replace $\lfloor x/d \rfloor$ with the continuous ratio $x/d$. This introduces an error term, but it reveals the main term:

\pi(x) \approx \pi(a) - 1 + x \sum_{d \mid P} \frac{\mu(d)}{d}

The Algebraic Bridge: Euler's Product

Notice the sum over $d \mid P$ with the Möbius function. There is a famous algebraic identity for the sum of $\mu(d)/d$ over divisors of $P$:

\sum_{d \mid P} \frac{\mu(d)}{d} = \prod_{p \le a} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{p} \right)

Proof of Identity:
Expand the product $(1 - 1/2)(1 - 1/3)(1 - 1/5)...$
You get: $1 - (\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5}) + (\frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{15}) - ...$
This exactly matches the definition of the Möbius sum: terms with an even number of primes get $+$, odd number get $-$.

Therefore, our approximate count becomes:

\pi(x) \approx x \prod_{p \le a} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{p} \right)

The Final Step: Mertens' Theorem

Here is where the natural logarithm $\ln(x)$ enters the algebra. Mertens' 3rd Theorem states exactly that:

\prod_{p \le a} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{p} \right) \sim \frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\ln(a)} \quad \text{(where } \gamma \text{ is Euler's constant, } \approx 0.57721\text{)}

Since $a = \sqrt{x}$, we have $\ln(a) = \frac{1}{2} \ln(x)$. Substituting this back into our formula:

\pi(x) \approx x \cdot \frac{2e^{-\gamma}}{\ln(x)}

The constant $2e^{-\gamma}$ is approximately $1.1229$. This is the "sieve correction factor." Gauss noticed that the actual density of primes is slightly lower; the real constant is $1$. This is the Prime Number Theorem: $\pi(x) \sim x / \ln(x)$.

Summary Demonstration

Step Expression Meaning
1. Parametric GCD Test $\sum_{d \mid P} \mu(d) \lfloor x/d \rfloor$ Exact count of numbers coprime to all primes $\le a$.
2. Continuous Approximation $x \prod_{p \le a} (1 - 1/p)$ Replace discrete floor with continuous division.
3. Mertens' Limit $x \cdot \frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\ln(a)}$ Asymptotic behavior of the product of $(1-1/p)$.
4. Substitute $a=\sqrt{x}$ $\sim \frac{x}{\ln(x)}$ Result.

Conclusion: The parametric test formula is the Legendre Sieve sum. It uses $\text{GCD}(n, P)=1$ as its engine. When you take the limit of that sum as $x \to \infty$, the algebra forces the natural logarithm to appear in the denominator, uniting the two concepts.
