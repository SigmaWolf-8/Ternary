## 2. Wieferich Primes Base 2

A prime p is a **Wieferich prime (base 2)** if:
\[
2^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}
\]
That is, the exponent of 2 modulo p² is exactly p‑1 (or divides p‑1), instead of the expected p(p‑1) from Fermat's little theorem lifting to p².

For p = 1093:
- p−1 = 1092.
- We require \(2^{1092} \equiv 1 \pmod{1093^2}\).

**Historical note:** Wieferich proved in 1909 that if the first case of Fermat's Last Theorem fails for exponent p (i.e., there exist integers x, y, z not divisible by p with xᵖ + yᵖ = zᵖ), then p must be a Wieferich prime base 2. At that time, no such primes were known. In 1913, Meissner found p = 1093. In 1922, Beeger found p = 3511. To this day, **only these two Wieferich primes are known** below 6.7×10¹⁵ (extensive searches continue). Whether infinitely many exist is an open problem.

## 3. Connection to the First Case of Fermat's Last Theorem

Let p be an odd prime. The **first case** of FLT states: there are no integers x, y, z with p ∤ xyz such that xᵖ + yᵖ = zᵖ.

Wieferich's criterion (1909):
> If the first case fails for p, then p must satisfy \(2^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}\).

This is a **necessary condition** for a counterexample. Therefore, if a prime p is **not** a Wieferich prime base 2, the first case of FLT holds for p automatically. This drastically reduced the search space for potential counterexamples before Wiles' proof (which of course settled FLT completely). The criterion was later extended by Mirimanoff (1911) to base 3, and further criteria involve Bernoulli numbers and Kummer's class number conditions.

Thus, 1093 and 3511 are the **only primes below astronomical bounds that even pass the first hurdle** to possibly violate the first case. They are mathematical "needles in a haystack" of ordinary primes.

**Why does 2 matter?** In the cyclotomic integers ℤ[ζₚ] used in Kummer's approach, the failure of unique factorization is encoded in the class number hₚ. The prime 2 plays a special role in the structure of the class group, and Wieferich's condition emerges from studying the 2‑Sylow subgroup.
