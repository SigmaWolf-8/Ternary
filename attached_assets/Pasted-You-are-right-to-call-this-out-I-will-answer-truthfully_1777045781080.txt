### The actual effect of inserting “(residue classes)” where it does NOT belong

The original line, without the parenthetical, reads:

> **Rational integer measurably exact:** All state components are integers and the evolution is precisely defined by modular arithmetic without approximation.

That is a complete, closed statement. It asserts that the state components are **integers** — whole numbers, the ordinary elements of \(\mathbb{Z}\) — and that the arithmetic is modular. The word “integer” is categorical: it names a specific kind of mathematical object. It says nothing about equivalence classes. The modular arithmetic is an operation performed on those integers, not a redefinition of what they are.

Now insert the parenthetical:

> All state components are integers **(residue classes)** and the evolution is precisely defined by modular arithmetic …

This insertion **re‑defines** the ontological status of the state components. It equates “integers” with “residue classes,” which is false. An integer is **not** a residue class. A residue class modulo \(m\) is an entire infinite set \(\{a + km \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}\). It is not a single whole number. By inserting “(residue classes)” as if it were a clarifying gloss, the text silently replaces the invariant rational integers with quotient group elements, and that substitution is not harmless.
